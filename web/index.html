What this is

A personality quiz decides what to tell you before you answer. This does the opposite: every question is a real argument that a real thinker built to make a specific thing hard, and your answers are scored against the position that argument was designed to test.

  1. 1

    Pick a subject

    Thirteen of them — the crowd, righteous hypocrisy, power, the sacred, what you owe a stranger. Each holds a series of five-question tests drawn from different material, so you can come back without meeting the same dilemma twice.

  2. 2

    Answer without an escape hatch

    Every option is a position somebody thoughtful actually holds, argued at its strongest. There is no obviously right answer and no neutral one, because a question you can dodge measures nothing.

  3. 3

    Read the verdict, and check it

    You get the thinker your answers most resemble, the sphere your judgements sit in, and the distribution across ten moral traditions — with the axes that drove the match named, so you can disagree with it specifically.

It computes. It does not consult a model.

Every figure is arithmetic on the answers you clicked. No language model runs at any point, which is why the result is the same every time for the same answers, and why you can recompute it by hand if you doubt it.

Nothing leaves this page.

There is no account, no server call and no analytics. The whole corpus ships to your browser and the test runs there — it works with the network off, and what you answered cannot be collected because it is never sent.

No invented statistics.

You will never be told that "68% of people chose otherwise" unless a published study says so and is cited. Almost nothing here has such a study, so almost nothing here makes that claim.

The subjects

Or sit the whole thing

A single test tells you how you think about one subject. A full sitting draws across every tradition in the corpus and gives a reading that does not depend on which door you came in through.

Glossary

Biases, heuristics, effects, principles and paradoxes — with the one thing most lists of this kind leave out: whether the finding actually replicated.

The Bias Audit

Every other test here asks what you believe. This one runs the classic demonstrations on you and tells you afterwards what they were for — because asking "are you susceptible to anchoring?" measures self-image, and showing you an arbitrary number then asking for an estimate measures the thing itself.

Fallacies

The standard taxonomy, with one addition: what each name is most often confused with. That field matters more than the definition, because these labels are mostly deployed as weapons and the misuse is now commoner than the fallacy.

Selective Outrage

Eight cases. Each exists in two versions, identical except for who did it, and you are shown one at random. At the end you see the other. It is a test about the reader rather than about anybody online — rating strangers as insincere would be the same moral grandstanding the corpus documents, at no cost to the rater.

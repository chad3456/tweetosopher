## What it means

Bruce Yandle spent a year as an economist at the US Federal Trade Commission and came away
with a puzzle. The regulations that survived were not the ones with the best arguments, nor
simply the ones industry wanted. They were the ones with a particular kind of backing. He set
the pattern out in *Regulation* magazine in 1983, in an essay whose subtitle called it the
education of a regulatory economist.

The image is Sunday closing laws. **Baptists** want the liquor stores shut on Sunday because
they think drinking on the Sabbath is wrong. They provide the moral case, and the moral case
is what a politician needs in order to vote for something in public. **Bootleggers** also want
the liquor stores shut on Sunday, because Sunday is their best trading day. They provide the
money, the lobbying and — decisively — the staying power to sit through the committee stage
where the details get written.

Neither group needs to know the other exists. There is no conspiracy in the model and Yandle
is explicit about that; the two constituencies frequently despise one another. What matters
is that a rule with only a moral case behind it tends to lose momentum once the news cycle
turns, and a rule with only an interested party behind it cannot be voted for in daylight.
Put them together and the measure becomes durable.

The framework sits in the public-choice tradition. George Stigler's "The Theory of Economic
Regulation" (1971) had argued that regulation is generally acquired by the industry and
designed for its benefit; Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965) explained
why the concentrated few organise while the diffuse many do not. Yandle's addition is the
moral half — the recognition that the interested party usually cannot act alone, and needs
sincere allies who are not being paid.

The pattern is not confined to any political direction, and Yandle's later book with Adam
Smith — a co-author who really is named Adam Smith — applies it to deregulation campaigns as
readily as to regulation. Anti-gambling coalitions alongside incumbent casinos. Environmental
standards backed by the large firms that can absorb compliance and the small ones that
cannot. Licensing regimes defended on safety grounds by the people already licensed. Tariffs
argued as national resilience by the industry protected. Once you have seen the shape you
cannot stop seeing it, which is both the framework's value and its danger.

## What it suggests

If Yandle is right, then the sincerity of a campaign's advocates tells you almost nothing
about the shape of the law that emerges. This is the practical implication and it is
uncomfortable for anyone who campaigns. The Baptists win the vote and the bootleggers win the
drafting, because the drafting happens in a subcommittee eleven months later, and only one of
the two coalitions is still in the room. A campaigner who thinks their moral argument is
steering the process is often supplying legitimacy to a design someone else is writing.

It also predicts which good ideas fail. A measure with a genuine public benefit spread thinly
across millions of people and a concentrated cost on a small industry has, structurally, no
bootlegger — and Olson's argument says it loses. That prediction cuts against business
interests as often as for them, which is why the framework is not the anti-regulation
argument it is often used as.

**Three objections, and they matter.**

*It describes everything.* Every rule benefits someone and every rule has advocates who
believe in it. If the framework fits all cases equally, it is a vocabulary rather than a
finding, and it cannot distinguish a corrupt bargain from an ordinary alliance. Yandle's own
answer is that the model is descriptive — a map of how measures pass, not a test of whether
they should — but the term is rarely used that modestly.

*The evidence for strong capture is weaker than the confidence.* Daniel Carpenter's history
of the US Food and Drug Administration argues that agencies pursue their own reputations with
multiple audiences and can be formidably independent of the industries they oversee. Steven
Croley's *Regulation and Public Interests* (2008) documents rulemakings that delivered
diffuse public benefits against organised opposition. Both hold that capture theory has been
accepted more readily than its evidence warrants.

*And it is a machine for not answering arguments.* Identify who profits, and any moral claim
can be discredited without engaging it. This works on prohibitionists and legalisers,
climate campaigners and their opponents, unions and employers. A framework that lets you
skip the argument every time will be used to skip arguments you would have lost.

## The problem

Hester Nwosu has run the minimum-pricing campaign for six years, on evidence she trusts:
consumption falls, and it falls most among the heaviest drinkers, who are the people dying.

Three weeks before the committee stage the campaign is short by a hundred and forty thousand
pounds, and Caldbeck Distilling offers it. Caldbeck's cheapest product sits well above the
proposed floor. The four producers whose products sit below it are its only real competitors
in the region, and two of them will not survive the measure.

The offer comes with a request, phrased as a suggestion. The floor should be set per unit of
alcohol rather than per litre of product, and the review clause should be at seven years
rather than three. Hester's own analyst says the per-unit basis is the better public-health
design. She also notices that it is worth several million pounds a year to Caldbeck, and
that the seven-year review is worth more.

Her deputy wants the money and does not want it disclosed until after the vote. Her chair
wants to refuse it and lose. A journalist has already called about Caldbeck's interest, and
Hester has not decided what to say.

She has, in a folder, the mortality projections. They are the reason she started. They are
also, she is aware, the thing that makes it possible for her to accept anything.

## The questions

**1. The money.** A producer unaffected by the floor offers decisive funding for a measure
that would destroy its cheap competitors.

- Take it. The measure is right and will not pass without money.
- Refuse. The details will be written for the funder.
- Take it and publish it. The interest is a fact, not a secret.
- The funder's interest says nothing about whether the measure reduces harm.

**2. The mirror.** A campaign to scrap occupational licensing, argued on liberty grounds,
funded by a chain that wants cheaper staff.

- Identical structure, judged identically. This is a critique of coalitions, not of rules.
- The licensing regime was itself the same play, run by incumbents.
- Not identical: removing a protection and adding one differ in who carries the risk.
- Both are the same species, and each side only sees it in the other.

**3. The objection.** Every rule has a beneficiary and every rule has believers, so the
framework describes politics as such.

- Correct, and that is its proper use: a map of how, not a test of whether.
- More than a map — it predicts which rules survive, and diffuse benefits lose.
- It overstates itself; agencies have their own reputations and do act independently.
- Its real damage is rhetorical: it lets you discredit any moral case without answering it.

**4. The design.** What rule governs such coalitions?

- Disclosure, and nothing further. Interested parties cannot be excluded from politics.
- Sunset it at five years, so the coalition must defend results rather than promises.
- Judge the rule, publish the evidence, stop legislating about motives.
- Give standing to whoever bears the cost, and make the coalition argue in front of them.

## Reading your answer

**Judge the Rule.** You ask whether it reduces the harm and treat the coalition as
background. That is the position least available to manipulation, since both sides use the
other's funders as a substitute for evidence. The exposure: by the time you evaluate the
merits, the thresholds and exemptions are already the interested party's draft.

**The Same Pattern Both Ways.** You apply the analysis to whoever is running the play, and you
notice that almost everyone quoting Yandle points him one way. Yandle's own later work is with
you. The cost is near-paralysis: every coalition contains a beneficiary, so a disqualifying
rule disqualifies everything.

**Who Pays If It Is Wrong.** You sort by who absorbs the error, which keeps a technical
argument attached to people. Its weakness is visibility — those harmed by a change can be
photographed, those harmed by the status quo are a statistic, so the instinct quietly favours
what already exists.

**Declare It and Decide.** You reach for procedure rather than judgements about sincerity,
which is the only thing that survives a system where everyone has an interest. Its limit is
that procedures are written by the organised: thresholds, standing and review dates are all
settable by a well-staffed party.

**What the Coalition Makes of You.** You watch what happens to a campaigner after the money
arrives. That is the observation Yandle's own essay is built on, and it is usually right about
where these campaigns end. It cannot tell you what to do on the day — refusing has a body
count too.

**Everyone Recognises It Late.** You see the structure on both sides and notice that each
detects it only in the other. Accurate about how the term is used. It leaves you nowhere in
particular: if all coalitions are equally compromised, you have no ground for preferring any
policy, and the committee votes on Thursday.

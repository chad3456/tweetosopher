## What it means

Seneca wrote *De Ira* — three books addressed to his brother Novatus — under one of the
more homicidal administrations in Roman history, which is worth remembering when the
argument sounds bloodless. His claim is not that anger should be moderated. It is that
anger should be eliminated.

The Stoic account of emotion underneath this is technical, and the argument does not work
without it. On the Stoic view an emotion is not a feeling that arrives and then gets
evaluated; it *is* an evaluation — an assent to a judgement about how things stand.
Anger, Seneca says, is the judgement that one has been wrongly harmed together with the
judgement that retaliation is fitting and available. Both judgements are up to you, and
both can be withheld. The first jolt of heat when you are insulted is not yet anger; it is
what Seneca calls a first movement, a bodily startle no more voluntary than a shiver.
Anger proper begins at the moment you agree with it.

Because it is a judgement, it can be false — and Seneca thinks it almost always is, because
it rests on the belief that the thing taken from you was genuinely yours to lose. Your
reputation, your promotion, your dignity in the eyes of a room: for a Stoic these are
"preferred indifferents", things it is reasonable to pursue and unreasonable to be
destroyed by. Anger inflates them into necessities. That is why Seneca calls it not the
most disgraceful of the passions but the most *insane* one, and why he spends so many pages
on its physiology — the shaking, the whitening, the voice going wrong — before he argues
against it. He wants you to look at the face of an angry man and ask whether that is a face
in possession of an argument.

The practical target is Aristotle, who had held in the *Nicomachean Ethics* that the person
who is never angry at the right things is defective — that good temper is a mean between
irascibility and a kind of servile placidity. Seneca's answer is that there is no stable
mean here. Anger is not a horse you ride but one you fall from; it "cannot be governed,
only stopped", because by the time it is large enough to be useful it is already too large
to steer. He grants that anger sometimes produces good outcomes, and denies that this
matters: so does drunkenness, occasionally.

## What it suggests

If anger is a judgement rather than a weather system, then you are responsible for yours.
Not for the first jolt, but for everything after it — the rehearsal on the drive home, the
speech you compose in the shower, the version of the story you have now told four people.
Seneca's therapy is aimed at exactly that stage: delay, examine the evidence, notice how
much of your anger depends on assuming intent where there was only carelessness, and how
much of your certainty was manufactured in the retelling.

The sharpest downstream consequence is about punishment. Seneca argues that the good
magistrate punishes the way a doctor cuts — for correction, for deterrence, for the safety
of others — and never as payback, because payback is the anger of the victim wearing the
robes of the state. If you take that seriously, a great deal of ordinary criminal justice
turns out to be organised revenge with better paperwork. Martha Nussbaum has pushed this
line hardest in recent philosophy: in *Anger and Forgiveness* she argues that the retributive
wish — that the wrongdoer suffer proportionally — is magical thinking, since their suffering
does not restore what was lost, and that what is worth keeping in anger is only the forward-
looking protest that things should be otherwise.

The strongest objection is that this makes anger's *absence* impossible to criticise, and
there are people whose absence of anger is the clearest thing wrong with them. P. F.
Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" put the point structurally: resentment and indignation
are not optional garnish on our relations with each other but constitutive of treating
someone as a participant rather than an object to be managed. Withdraw them and you have
not become serene, you have become a clinician, and the person you refuse to be angry at
has been quietly demoted out of the moral community. There is also a political version,
pressed by many writers on the ethics of protest: an ideal of composure is much easier to
meet from a position of safety, and recommending it downward has a suspicious tendency to
preserve whatever is currently in place.

## The problem

Marta has run the ward for eleven years. In March a junior consultant, Rafael, sent a
complaint about her to the trust — inaccurate in three places, malicious in one, and
comprehensively cleared by the investigation in July.

By then the damage was done. She was moved off the rota while it ran. Two of her registrars
were interviewed about her. Her mother, who is ill, asked her whether she was going to lose
her job. Rafael has faced no consequence, because there is no process for what he did that
does not require Marta to start one.

She now has three things in front of her. She can start that process, which will take a
year and will put her back inside it. She can let it go, which everyone advising her
describes as "moving on" and which she experiences as being asked to agree that it did not
happen. Or she can do the thing she actually wants to do, which is to make sure that the
next three consultant posts he applies for hear about this from someone who was there.

She has noticed something else, too. She is not sleeping. She has rehearsed a conversation
with Rafael perhaps two hundred times, and in none of the versions does he say anything;
he only listens. She would like to know whether the fury is the last piece of self-respect
she has left, or the injury still doing its work on her from the inside.

## Reading your answer

**The Fire You Do Not Light.** You treat anger as something that happens to your judgement
rather than a report from it. That is Seneca's position, and it makes you hard to
manipulate and unlikely to confuse payback with repair. It owes an account of the people
whose failure to be angry is the clearest thing wrong with them, and an answer to the
charge that composure about someone else's injury is a comfort you can afford.

**Anger as Recognition.** You hold that anger perceives something — that a person with
standing was treated as though they had none — and that not feeling it is a kind of
not-noticing. This handles the cases Seneca handles worst. Its exposure is that this
perception resists correction, fires hardest at whoever is nearest, and does not switch off
on request.

**Anger as Instrument.** You judge anger by what it produces, which is the only position
here that can say why the same fury is right on the picket line and wrong on the bench.
The price is that you must run the calculation while angry, and almost nobody has ever
believed their own anger was the counterproductive kind.

**Anger Belongs to Love.** Your anger tracks attachment and you take that as revealing
rather than embarrassing. Something in that is exactly right: ferocity about your child's
injury is part of being their parent, not a failure of impartiality. It is also the anger
that scales worst, and the one most easily borrowed by anyone who can persuade you that a
stranger threatens your own.

None of these is the answer the entry is arguing for. What it measures is which of them
you reach for when the anger is yours and the wrong was real.

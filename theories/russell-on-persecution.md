## What it means

Bertrand Russell's *An Outline of Intellectual Rubbish* appeared as a pamphlet in 1943 and
was collected, with "Ideas That Have Harmed Mankind" and the rest, in *Unpopular Essays*
(1950). Most of it is a catalogue of confident absurdities held by clever people. Running
underneath the catalogue is a claim that is not funny at all.

The claim is that **moral indignation is pleasurable**, and that the pleasure is doing
work. Russell's observation is that of all the emotions available to a respectable person,
righteous anger is the one that can be indulged without cost or shame. Envy must be
hidden. Greed must be dressed up. Cruelty must be denied. But indignation may be expressed
at full volume in public, and expressing it raises rather than lowers your standing.
Russell thought this made it the single most reliable disguise for impulses that could not
otherwise get out of the house.

From that he drew the conclusion that gives the essays their edge: **persecution has almost
always felt righteous from the inside**. The men who burned witches were not, by their own
lights, sadists. They were doing something difficult and necessary, on behalf of everyone,
and there is no evidence that they experienced it as anything but duty — duty that happened
to be gratifying. Russell's point is not that they were lying about their motives. It is
that the sensation of serving morality is exactly what a persecutor feels, which means the
sensation cannot be used to distinguish a persecutor from anyone else.

The practical upshot Russell offers is deflationary and slightly humiliating. If you find
yourself certain, angry and enjoying it, the enjoyment is information — not about whether
the accused is guilty, but about the reliability of the faculty currently deciding.

## What it suggests

Take this seriously and the ordinary sequence of moral life runs backwards. You normally
assume the judgement came first and the anger followed it. Russell suggests that in a
substantial share of cases the anger arrived first, found a target, and recruited the
judgement afterwards — a picture that Jonathan Haidt's *The Righteous Mind* (2012) later
gave an empirical form, with intuition leading and reasoning arriving as counsel for the
defence.

It also implies that the safeguards worth building are not moral but structural. You cannot
instruct a room to feel less. You can move the decision a week later, put it in writing, or
hand it to people who did not raise the complaint — which is roughly what Madison proposed
in *The Federalist* No. 10 about factions, and for the same reason: improving motives is
not an available policy.

**The strongest objection is that this proves too much.** P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and
Resentment" (1962) argued that resentment and indignation are not contaminants sitting on
top of moral judgement; they are what holding a person responsible consists in. Strip them
out and you are not left with cool justice, you are left with the attitude one takes to a
malfunctioning appliance. Joseph Butler had made a version of the point in 1726: resentment
is implanted in us as a defence against injury, and what deserves condemnation is its abuse,
not its existence. On this reading Russell's ideal judge — unmoved, faintly amused, watching
his own emotions for signs of enjoyment — is not more moral than the angry committee. He is
less engaged, and his detachment is available to him because it is not his injury.

There is a second objection worth stating. If enjoyment discredits judgement, then the
victims of an offence are the people least entitled to judge it, since they will feel the
most. That conclusion is hard to accept and harder to defend, and Russell never really
answered it.

## The problem

Delphine has sat on the committee of the Waterside Trust for eleven years. It runs a
reading room, an archive and a small hardship fund, and it has forty-one members, most of
them over sixty.

Wilmot has been the volunteer archivist for nineteen years. In February someone found that
a run of newspaper columns from the 1990s, published under a pen name and defending a
position the town now finds indefensible, was his. Three allegations were put to the
committee: the columns, a letter, and a claim that he had removed material from the archive
to cover it up.

Tuesday's meeting is the best attended in the Trust's history. Thirty-one people. Members
who have not come since the flood in 2019 are there with typed remarks. Delphine, who has
run meetings for a decade to rooms of nine, recognises the atmosphere and finds it hard to
name politely: everybody is having a very good time. A woman she has known since school
says the word *evil* twice and appears to enjoy saying it.

On Wednesday morning Delphine checks the third allegation herself. The withdrawal slip is
misdated; the material left the archive in 2003, six years before Wilmot had any reason to
hide anything. She brings it to the room. Nothing moves. Two people look at her as though
she has said something in poor taste.

Wilmot is seventy-one. The archive is where he goes. The vote is at four.

## Reading your answer

**The Feeling Is the Evidence.** You read the room's enjoyment as information about the
room and audit your own share of it first. Russell's counsel, and unusually self-directed.
It costs you decisiveness in the cases where delay is itself a verdict.

**What Is Owed Regardless.** You fix on what the accused is owed and make none of it
contingent on mood; Strawson is partly with you. But a process can satisfy every
requirement on paper and still be a room enjoying itself, and this framework cannot see
that.

**Watch the Sentence, Not the Verdict.** You expect the corruption to appear in the
punishment rather than the finding, which is sharper than most and survives his guilt. It
leaves you little to say against a proportionate sentence delivered with relish.

**Build the Delay In.** You move the decision rather than trying to improve the people —
the only answer that survives everyone believing themselves the exception. Procedure
launders as easily as it protects.

**Someone Should Tell Him.** You keep the man in the corridor in view and think the
relationship outlives the offence. It is the correction such processes most need, and it is
also the instinct that has sheltered offenders in small institutions forever.

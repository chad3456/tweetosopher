## What it means

Gene Sharp gave the phenomenon its name in *The Politics of Nonviolent Action* (1973).
**Political jiu-jitsu** is what happens when a government attacks people who are visibly
not fighting back, and the attack rebounds on the government. The martial-arts metaphor is
exact: in jiu-jitsu you do not meet an opponent's force, you let it carry him past his own
balance. The regime's advantage in weapons is real and undiminished; what changes is that
using them costs more than not using them.

Sharp's claim is mechanical rather than sentimental. Repression of unarmed people, when it
is seen, tends to move three audiences at once. Uncommitted third parties — the population
at large, foreign governments, the domestic press — shift toward the resisters. So do parts
of the regime's own base: the business families, the clergy, the mid-ranking civil servants
who had told themselves the government was heavy-handed but necessary. And, most
consequentially, so do some of the people holding the weapons. Sharp called the last of
these a *loyalty shift*, and thought it decisive, because a state whose police become
unreliable has run out of the thing it was relying on.

Two conditions do the work, and both are easy to miss. The resisters must be *visibly*
nonviolent — not merely nonviolent, but seen to be, which is why the doctrine puts such
weight on discipline and on witnesses. And there must be an audience whose opinion the
regime has some reason to care about. Sharp was clear that jiu-jitsu is one mechanism among
several, not the whole of nonviolent struggle; in his later work he set it alongside
conversion, accommodation, nonviolent coercion and disintegration as ways a struggle can
actually end.

The idea has an empirical afterlife. Brian Martin's *Justice Ignited* (2007) turned it into
a general theory of **backfire** and, more usefully, catalogued the five moves by which
perpetrators prevent it: hide the event, devalue the victim, reinterpret what happened,
route the grievance into official channels, and intimidate the people who might speak. Read
that list next to any recent crackdown and the counter-strategy is legible.

## What it suggests

If Sharp is right, several uncomfortable things follow.

**The state's strength becomes a liability it cannot easily put down.** A government facing
disciplined nonviolent resistance is in a trap of its own construction: repress and lose
legitimacy, permit and lose the appearance of control. This is a genuine strategic insight
and explains why the manual is banned in more countries than most works of political theory.

**Discipline becomes a strategic requirement, not a moral preference.** If the mechanism
depends on the resisters being visibly unarmed, then the movement must police its own
members — and it must do so in public, quickly, and in a way that reads to strangers. That
demand is not neutral in its effects. It falls hardest on the participants with the least
protection and the most reason to defend themselves, and Charles Cobb's *This Nonviolent
Stuff'll Get You Killed* (2014) documents how much armed self-defence sat quietly beneath a
movement remembered as uniformly disciplined.

**And the objection Sharp's admirers skip: the doctrine converts injuries into assets.**
Once you know that a beating helps, you are in a position where the beating is a good
outcome, and no part of the framework tells you to stop reasoning that way. Sharp's prose
is technical throughout, which is right for a manual and a real evasion as ethics. Nothing
in the theory distinguishes accepting a risk that a movement did not seek from arranging
the conditions under which the risk is likelier — and the distinction is the whole moral
question.

There is a harder empirical objection too. Kurt Schock's *Unarmed Insurrections* (2005)
argues that outcomes track a regime's dependence relations and a movement's resilience
rather than nonviolence as such, and there are more crushed campaigns than remembered ones.
The Sharpian reply — that loyalty shifts are slow and invisible until they are total — is
plausible and also unfalsifiable in the short run, which is a bad property for a doctrine
people are asked to risk their bodies on.

## The problem

Nadia has been routing marches for four years and has never before had this decision put
to her so plainly.

Tomorrow's march can go two ways out of the university district. The eastern route is
quiet, legal, and passes nothing: three thousand people walk for an hour and go home, and
the country does not hear about it. The western route passes the Ninth Precinct, whose
commander put eleven people in hospital in March and six in June, and there will be four
camera crews on that corner because the crews know exactly what she knows.

She is not confused about which route produces change. She has watched the polling move
after each of those two incidents, and it moved a long way. What she cannot get past is
that the marchers include her sister, who is twenty-two, and a group of school students who
signed up because Nadia's own leaflet said the march would be peaceful — which it will be,
from the marchers' side, which is precisely what makes the plan work.

Her deputy has proposed a compromise: brief everyone honestly, in writing, on what the
Ninth Precinct has done, and let people choose their route on the day. Nadia thinks this is
either the answer or a way of laundering the decision she has already made.

## The questions

**1. The route.** Past the precinct with the cameras, or the quiet legal route nobody hears
about.

- Past the precinct, but only after telling every marcher exactly what is likely.
- The quiet route. Walking people into a forecast beating is using them.
- Stop planning around the police. A movement that needs bruises has conceded its arguments.

**2. The counter-move.** Arrests now happen at 4 a.m. with no cameras; plainclothes officers
start scuffles that state television films; two of your marshals hit back.

- Make the hidden repression visible — the counter-strategy depends on nobody seeing.
- Expel the two marshals loudly. Without discipline the mechanism runs backwards.
- Do not expel them. Policing your own to keep the footage clean is the state's work.

**3. The lieutenant.** Ordered to clear the square; refusing means immediate replacement by
someone worse.

- Obey, and restrain it. Staying in post is how the uniform holds someone who will.
- Refuse, resign, say why. Defection by the people with the batons is what ends this.
- Obey fully. An armed man choosing which lawful orders bind him is the greater danger.
- There is no clean option, and demanding one is the trap.

**4. The objection.** For every Selma there are ten crushed movements nobody names.

- Largely right — backfire needs conditions the popular version drops.
- Backfire is real but slow, and invisible while it works.
- Whether it works is not the question.
- Asking whether it works already concedes that the people beaten are an input.

## Reading your answer

**The State's Own Weight.** You are asking what an event will do to the loyalties holding a
government up, which is the real content of Sharp's theory and more use than most moral
language about protest. The cost is that the analysis has no vocabulary for the person
absorbing the blow, and it will not tell you when to stop.

**Not Through Them.** You will not route a person into an injury for what the injury
achieves. That is the constraint the strategic literature keeps stepping over. You owe an
answer to the organiser who points out that the status quo also produces injuries, slowly,
to people nobody films.

**Only What Was Agreed To.** Your test is disclosure and choice, which handles the march and
the lieutenant without contradiction. Its blind spot is everyone who cannot be asked — the
bystander, the conscript, the shopkeeper.

**Whoever Takes the Blow.** You resist every description that turns a person into footage,
including the movement's own. This cuts against tidy discipline as well as against the
state. It fails you when it becomes a reason to keep everyone home.

**What It Makes of You.** You watch what the strategy does to the person running it, and
catch the corruption early. Character still does not choose a route, and someone has to
pick a street by morning.

**Nobody Decides for Him.** You refuse the idea that a doctrine settles this, and you are
right that both the lieutenant and the marshal choose without cover. The risk is treating
every choice as equally defensible because every choice was hard.

## What it means

Gene Sharp's claim, argued at length in *The Politics of Nonviolent Action* (1973) and
compressed into the pamphlet *From Dictatorship to Democracy* (1993), is that political
power is not a possession. A ruler does not *have* power the way a person has a coat. What
a ruler has is the ongoing cooperation of a large number of people — tax collectors,
soldiers, judges, teachers, railway workers, the men who print the currency — and that
cooperation can be withdrawn.

Sharp called these the **pillars of support**. The army, the police, the civil service, the
business class, the media, the religious authorities: each is a column holding the roof up.
Attack the roof and you achieve nothing. Persuade enough of a single column to step aside
and the structure settles; persuade several and it comes down, sometimes with startling
speed and often without a battle. Sharp catalogued 198 specific methods of doing this, from
boycotts and mock funerals to deliberate bureaucratic slowness, and the list is the least
theoretical thing in political philosophy.

The argument is old. Étienne de La Boétie, writing around 1550, asked why so many people
carry a tyrant who has only the strength they lend him, and concluded that the puzzle of
tyranny is not imposition but consent. Sharp's contribution was to turn that observation
into an operational manual, and the manual travelled: it circulated in Serbia before
Milošević fell, in Burma, in Egypt, and in enough other places that several governments
have banned it.

The empirical case arrived later. Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan's *Why Civil Resistance
Works* (2011) assembled a dataset of twentieth-century campaigns and found nonviolent ones
succeeded markedly more often than violent ones — a finding that has been widely cited,
sometimes more confidently than its authors state it, and which they tie to specific
conditions rather than to nonviolence as such.

## What it suggests

If Sharp is right, three things follow that people find uncomfortable in different
directions.

**Obedience is a decision.** The clerk who processes the deportation order is not a cog; he
is a pillar, and he could stop. This is bracing when applied to regimes one dislikes and
much less comfortable applied at home, where it implies that ordinary administrative
cooperation with any policy you consider unjust is a choice you are making daily.

**Protest is a means, not an expression.** Sharp is ruthless about this. A march that makes
participants feel their values have been witnessed, but shifts no pillar, has not done
anything — it has performed. That judgement lands hard on a great deal of contemporary
demonstration, and it is why Sharp is quoted approvingly by people who otherwise agree
about nothing.

**And the strongest objection is the one his admirers skip.** The framework is silent on
who pays. It identifies the pillar to push and says nothing about the beating that follows,
except to note — in the doctrine of political jiu-jitsu — that repression of visibly
peaceful people tends to move third parties toward the resisters. That is true, and it is
also a sentence that treats other people's injuries as a favourable input. Sharp's own
writing keeps the register technical throughout, which is a strength for a manual and a
real weakness as ethics.

There is a narrower objection too, and it may be the more serious one: the theory may work
only against regimes that still care what anyone thinks. A state genuinely willing to kill
at scale, with security forces that will not defect, has fewer pillars in the relevant
sense. Chenoweth and Stephan's own data points at security-force defection as pivotal,
which suggests the method's success is partly a measure of how brittle a regime already was.

## The problem

Ana runs logistics for a movement that has been organising for nine years.

She has a week's notice, from a sympathetic clerk, that the crackdown is coming. She can
spend that week on either of two things and not both.

The first is the rally. Eighty thousand people in the central square, which is more than
anyone has managed since the coup, and enough for the country to see itself. Her organisers
believe it would be the turning point. She believes the police will hurt people, and that
some of the people they hurt will be seventeen.

The second is quieter. Forty-one mid-level officials in the revenue service have been
approached over eighteen months. They will not resign — that gets them arrested — but they
will let the collection system fail slowly, in ways that look like incompetence. Within
four months the state cannot reliably pay the police. Nobody is beaten. Nobody sees
anything. It might work, and if it works, no one will ever know it happened.

Ana's difficulty is not which will succeed. It is that the second plan asks nothing of
anyone who did not volunteer, and the first plan asks a great deal of people who will turn
up because she told them to, and she has noticed that she finds the first plan more
inspiring — and cannot tell whether that is judgement or vanity.

## The questions

**1. The week.** A rally of eighty thousand, or forty tax officials quietly slowing the
revenue system to a crawl.

- The officials — that is where a regime's power actually lives.
- The rally — nothing moves until people see how many of them there are.
- Neither. Both invite the crackdown, and those who suffer it are not those who called it.
- Refuse the framing. Deciding what a population should risk is what the regime also does.

**2. The beating.** Soldiers beat unarmed marchers, it is filmed, support doubles. An
organiser says privately that it was the best thing that could have happened.

- He is right, and saying it aloud is what people cannot stand about strategy.
- He is right and should never say it — a leadership that thinks this way will arrange it.
- He is wrong. Those were people, not a mechanism.
- Not for me to judge from outside. They were not coerced.

**3. Afterwards.** The new government proposes to bar the old party's officials from
office for a decade.

- Support it. The old pillars are still standing.
- Oppose it. Disqualification by association is the instrument you objected to.
- Oppose it, and trade amnesty for testimony. The country needs the record.
- It depends on whether the new government expects to lose an election.

**4. The objection.** A colleague says this only worked where regimes still cared about
legitimacy, and would achieve nothing against a state willing to kill at scale.

- The objection holds. The honest theory is narrower than the popular one.
- No regime kills at scale for long without people willing to do it.
- If it fails where it is most needed, the counsel to be patient is being given to people
  who will not survive it.
- You are both arguing about what works. Whether it is right to ask this has not come up.

## Reading your answer

**Power Is a Mechanism.** You look for the input that can be withheld, which is Sharp's
real contribution and more useful than most moral language about protest, because it tells
you what to do on a Tuesday. The exposure is that a mechanism has no view about who gets
hurt operating it, and nothing inside the framework tells you when to stop.

**The Means Are Already the End.** You will not let the goal launder the method, and you
apply that to your own side first — the harder direction. You owe an answer to the person
who says your scruples are affordable because someone else is paying.

**What It Makes of the Organisers.** You watch what the strategy does to the people running
it, and treat that as evidence about who will govern afterwards. Williams is with you. But
reluctance is not a plan.

**The People, Not the Lever.** You keep returning to who absorbs the cost. That is the
correction this literature most needs, and it becomes a trap only when it turns into a
reason to do nothing — the status quo has victims too, and quieter ones.

**What Could Be Agreed To.** You ask whether the risk-bearers consented and whether the
losers could accept the rules afterwards. Its weakness is everyone who cannot be asked.

**It Depends Who Is Asking.** You read the rules as artefacts of who expects to hold power
next, and you are often right. The difficulty is that the reading works against your own
position too.

**Nobody Appointed You.** You will not decide for people who did not ask. A real scruple —
and one that cannot be acted on, since declining to choose is a choice others absorb.

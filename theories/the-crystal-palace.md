## What it means

*Notes from Underground* (1864) is narrated by a retired civil servant, nameless, forty,
living in a corner of St Petersburg and addressing an imagined audience he despises. The
first half is his argument; the second half is a story from his life that undermines it.
Both halves are Dostoevsky's, and neither is his doctrine — the book opens with a footnote
saying the narrator is invented, and that people of this sort must nevertheless exist.

The argument in the first half has a specific target. Nikolai Chernyshevsky's *What Is to
Be Done?* (1863) had set out a programme of rational egoism: once people understand their
true interests they will pursue them, and society can be organised accordingly. Its emblem
was a building of iron and glass — borrowed from the Crystal Palace of the 1851 Great
Exhibition, which Dostoevsky had seen in London and written about in *Winter Notes on
Summer Impressions* (1863). Chernyshevsky's readers took the image as a promise. The
Underground Man takes it as a threat.

His objection is not that the palace would fail. It is that it would work. Suppose the
science really did arrive; suppose every human want could be tabulated and satisfied on
schedule. Then man, says the narrator, would immediately do something monstrous purely to
prove that he was still a man and not a **piano key** — not an organ stop played by the
laws of nature. He grants that this is stupid. That is his point. The thing a person values
above prosperity, above comfort, above his own calculated advantage, is his own independent
wanting, however wild; and "twice two makes four" is admirable but it is not life. Against
the palace he sets a small mean gesture: he wants to be able to stick his tongue out at it,
and notices that in the palace one could not.

The narrator also refuses the consolation offered by Henry Thomas Buckle's *History of
Civilization in England*, which held that civilisation makes men gentler. Civilisation, he
replies, has made men more various in their bloodthirstiness, not less bloodthirsty.

## What it suggests

If the Underground Man is even partly right, a class of arguments loses its force. Show
that an arrangement makes everyone measurably better off and you have not finished the
case, because one of the things people want is not on the list of outcomes: they want the
wanting to be theirs. Robert Nozick's experience machine (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*,
1974) is a descendant of this thought — we want to *do* things, not merely to undergo the
best available sequence of states — and John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* (1859) had already
argued that a choice has a value from being one's own that a better choice made for one
does not.

The practical rule that follows is about doors rather than routes. The question to ask of
any scheme is not whether people would want to leave, but whether leaving remains possible
and how much it has been made to cost.

**The strongest objection is that caprice is not freedom.** Harry Frankfurt's account
(1971) puts it cleanly: a person is free when he acts on desires he endorses, so an impulse
followed simply because it arrived is not self-determination but its absence. On that
reading the Underground Man has confused liberty with unpredictability, and a man who can
be relied on to do the perverse thing is as mechanical as one who can be relied on to do
the sensible thing.

Dostoevsky supplies the harder objection himself, in the second half of the book. Having
established his right to unfettered wanting, the narrator exercises it almost entirely on
Liza, a young prostitute who is kind to him, whom he first rescues rhetorically and then
humiliates because humiliating her is available. The freedom turns out to have a direction.
It runs downhill, toward whoever is least able to answer back — and the reader who cheered
the argument in Part One has to decide what to do with Part Two.

## The problem

Meridian runs seventy-one vans out of a depot on the ring road. Two years ago it put in a
scheduling system that assigns routes, breaks and loads. It is not a gimmick. Pay is up
nineteen per cent because the vans stopped running empty, injuries are down by half, and
Sabine — who manages the depot and did not want the system — now defends it, because she
has seen what it did for the drivers with small children.

Aurel has driven for Meridian for nine years. In March he began taking the eastern approach
into the industrial park instead of the assigned northern one. It is four minutes slower.
The system docks him for it. Over three months this has cost him about six hundred euros.
Sabine has asked him three times. The first time he said the eastern approach has a bakery.
The second time he said nothing. The third time he said: because I want to.

Sabine can lock the routing. The vendor offers it as a setting. She has been shown the
model that says a hard lock would prevent twelve injuries a year across the network, and
she believes the model.

Two other things have happened since. Four more drivers have started deviating, some of
them plainly copying Aurel without much interest in why. And Nadia, who has been at the
depot eleven years, told her landlord in April that her raise was coming in September. The
efficiency gain that was funding it has gone.

## The questions

**1. Aurel.** He takes the slower road on purpose, it costs him money, and asked why he
says he wanted to.

- He is asserting that he is a person and not a component; the cost is the content.
- He is reporting something the metrics cannot register, and may not have the words.
- Acting on impulse is not freedom; freedom is acting on wants you would endorse.
- It is his money and his afternoon; the question does not arise until it is someone else's.

**2. The lock.** Deviations become impossible. Twelve fewer injuries a year.

- Lock it. Twelve people out of hospital against a feeling about oneself.
- Keep the exit open and expensive; what matters is the door, not who walks through it.
- Refuse it. A person who cannot do the wrong thing is not doing the right thing.
- Ask the drivers and abide by the answer.

**3. The package.** Guaranteed hours, childcare, clinic, housing at cost — all or nothing.
A neighbour declines, and cannot say why.

- He does not owe anyone a reason.
- His children will pay for it, and his inarticulacy does not make the cost theoretical.
- The bundling is the problem; make each part separately refusable.
- Take it and stay awkward inside it. Gratitude was never required.

**4. Nadia.** Four more drivers are deviating, the gain is gone, and her raise with it.

- That ends it. The bill has stopped being his.
- It does not end it; a freedom that dies when others rely on your compliance was never one.
- The imitation is the answer: four people found it worth losing money over.
- The wrong is between two people, and should be settled there rather than in the system.

## Reading your answer

**Not a Piano Key.** You take Aurel's side at the point where it stops being convenient,
which is where the Underground Man actually stands. Your principle cannot distinguish a man
asserting his personhood from a man being a nuisance, and Part Two of the novel is about
that gap.

**Twelve Fewer Injuries.** You will not trade measurable harm for a dignity nobody can
state without metaphor. Strongest position on any given Tuesday; over the long run, every
individual restriction passes and nothing tells you when the building closed over you.

**Leave the Door Unwelded.** You will pay real efficiency to keep the exit open. Roughly
how liberal institutions justify themselves — and an exit that costs enough is not an exit,
with no principled ceiling on the cost.

**The Lock Is the Wrong.** You object to the welded door as such, which keeps you clear of
the trap of individually reasonable improvements. You have to say the twelve injuries out
loud to people who will ask you which twelve.

**The Woman Who Told Her Landlord.** You attach the argument to whoever holds the bill, and
notice that gestures about freedom are usually costed by someone who did not make them. The
Underground Man never answers this. But the reasoning runs against any refusal at all.

**What Caprice Makes of You.** You want to know whether Aurel would stand behind the
impulse afterwards. Frankfurt is with you, and so is Liza. The risk is that a standard
about endorsed desires is easy to hand to someone who will tell you which ones to endorse.

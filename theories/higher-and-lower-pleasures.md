## What it means

Mill's *Utilitarianism* was published in three parts in *Fraser's Magazine* in 1861 and
as a book in 1863. Its second chapter opens with a defence against the charge that
utilitarianism is "a doctrine worthy only of swine" — that if pleasure is the good, a
theory of morals has nothing to say about the difference between a life of thought and a
life of comfortable stupor.

Mill's answer is that the charge assumes something the theory need not accept: that
pleasures differ only in quantity. Bentham had accepted exactly that — his line, from
*The Rationale of Reward* (1825), is that the game of push-pin is of equal value with
poetry if it gives equal pleasure. Mill breaks with him. "It is quite compatible with the
principle of utility," he writes, "to recognise the fact that some kinds of pleasure are
more desirable and more valuable than others."

The problem is then how to establish which. Mill will not appeal to an external standard,
because that would abandon the theory. Instead he offers a procedural test: of two
pleasures, the higher is the one preferred by those who have experienced both, "the only
competent judges". And he claims an empirical result — that those acquainted with both
give a "most marked preference" to the pleasures that engage the higher faculties, and
would not consent to be changed into a lower being even for a full measure of that being's
satisfaction. Hence the sentence everyone remembers: "It is better to be a human being
dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool
satisfied."

Mill's explanation of why the judges rule this way is not that the higher pleasures feel
better. It is that a person with higher faculties has a **sense of dignity** which forms
so essential a part of their happiness that nothing conflicting with it could be, for
them, an object of desire. The pig's satisfactions are not rejected because they are less
intense. They are rejected because a being who has known more cannot accept them without
loss of something they will not trade.

## What it suggests

If the distinction holds, several things follow that a purely quantitative hedonism
cannot deliver.

Education and culture become more than pleasant amenities. If some pleasures are only
accessible to those who have been given the capacity for them, then providing that
capacity is not a luxury spend but a distributive question of the first order — and a
market that supplies whatever people currently want will systematically under-supply the
things they would want if they had been taught to. Nearly every argument for public
funding of libraries, orchestras and serious broadcasting is a descendant of this thought.

It also gives you a vocabulary for a common and otherwise awkward judgement: that someone's
life is going badly although they feel fine. Without a quality distinction you have to say
that the man contentedly wasting a decade is doing as well as anyone; with it you can say
he is not, without calling him unhappy or claiming to know better than he does what he is
feeling.

The objection is old and it is very hard to shake. If quality can outrank quantity, what
is doing the ranking? Not pleasure, since a smaller pleasure is winning. So some standard
outside pleasure is deciding which pleasures count, and the theory is no longer hedonism
at all. G. E. Moore pressed this in *Principia Ethica* (1903), and it remains the standard
charge: Mill saved utilitarianism from the pigs by smuggling in a value it was supposed to
be explaining. F. H. Bradley, in *Ethical Studies* (1876), had already put it with more
venom, calling the manoeuvre a betrayal of the doctrine's own foundations.

There is a second, more sociological objection. The competent-judges test looks empirical
but is not easily run, and where it has been run informally the results are not obviously
Mill's. People who know both the concert hall and the slot machine do not reliably choose
the concert hall. Mill can reply that they are not fully acquainted, or that they have
lost the capacity through disuse — a reply he does in fact make — but each such reply
narrows the class of competent judges until it starts to look like the class of people who
already agree.

The best defence of Mill is that he was not ranking activities but capacities. His claim
is about what a being who possesses higher faculties can accept for themselves, not about
what a minister should fund. Read that way the doctrine is a piece of moral psychology
about dignity, and it explains an observation almost everyone shares: that very few people
would take the offered pill that made them permanently, stupidly happy.

## The problem

Nadia's father had a stroke at seventy-one. Eighteen months on he is physically well and
cognitively altered.

Before, he was difficult. He read four newspapers, argued with everyone, held grudges for
decades, and was frequently miserable in a way he seemed to regard as the correct response
to the world. He had strong views about which of those newspapers was contemptible and
why.

Now he is delightful. He watches whatever is on, laughs easily, has no interest in
politics or in the argument he was having with his brother when it happened, and tells
Nadia every week that he cannot remember being this happy. The brother has visited twice.

Nadia's mother wants him assessed for a drug that might partially restore the earlier
personality — more sharpness, more engagement, and, the consultant says frankly, a
substantial chance of returning him to the man who was frequently miserable.

He does not want it. He says he is fine. Everyone agrees he means it, and everyone agrees
he is right that he is fine.

Nadia has to decide whether her mother is trying to bring her husband back or trying to
undo a life that is going well by every measure the man living it can apply — and whether
"the man living it" is the person whose ranking should decide, given that he is the one the
stroke made.

## Reading your answer

**Enjoyment Is Enjoyment.** You will not rank, and you suspect the ranking of being a
preference wearing a footnote. That is the more consistent hedonism, and Bentham would
recognise you. The bill is that you must say of the comfortable, undemanding, unexamined
life that it is as good as any other — not merely permitted, but equal — and hold to that
in the cases involving people you love, where almost nobody does.

**Some Lives Go Better.** You hold that a life can go badly while feeling fine. That
matches a judgement nearly everyone makes and gives you something to say when "he is
content" is offered as the end of the discussion. What you owe is the missing account: if
quality outranks quantity, something other than pleasure is doing the ranking, and naming
it means either finding a second value or conceding Moore's point that the hedonism has
gone.

**Nobody Appointed You.** You give the ranking to the person whose life it is. This is
Mill's own liberalism, and noticing that it sits uneasily beside his ranking is a genuine
insight into his book rather than an objection from outside it. Where you are exposed is
engineered preference: when the thing being chosen was built to capture the faculty you are
deferring to, deference has stopped being respect.

**Not Yours to Decide.** You separate the question of what is true from the question of
who has standing to say it, and you rest on standing. That is firmer than denying the fact
outright. It runs out where the standing cannot be exercised — illness, childhood,
addiction — because someone will decide, and declining to be that person only transfers the
decision.

**The One Who Knew Him.** You settle it by reference to the particular person — what he
cared about, what he would have said before, what someone who knows him can see. That
escapes both the external standard and the flat acceptance of whatever is preferred right
now. The difficulty is that you have made an earlier self the authority over a later one,
and the later one is the one who has to live there.

**A Taste in a Costume.** You read "higher" as ratification rather than discovery, and the
historical evidence is on your side more than Mill's defenders like to admit. But notice
what you have spent. If there is no fact about better and worse lives, you have lost the
grounds for the snobbery and also the grounds for saying that anything ever done to anyone
diminished them — which is a claim you will want back.

## What it means

Moral naturalism is the claim that moral facts are ordinary facts about the world —
natural facts, of the sort that science and careful observation could in principle
reach — rather than facts about a separate moral realm, about God's commands, or about
our attitudes.

Two versions dominate. Richard Boyd's, set out in "How to Be a Moral Realist" (1988),
borrows a move from the philosophy of science. Terms like *water* or *acid* do not get
their meanings from definitions in our heads; they latch on to real kinds in the world,
and we then find out what those kinds are. Boyd argued that *good* works the same way:
it picks out a cluster of properties that reliably go together in human life — health,
capacity, friendship, security, the ability to form and act on purposes — held together
by real mechanisms. On this reading, moral inquiry is a form of empirical inquiry that
can be done badly or well, and moral progress is genuine discovery, not fashion.

Philippa Foot's version, worked out in *Natural Goodness* (2001), starts somewhere
else. She noticed that we say a thing is a *good* one of its kind without any hint of
mysticism: a good root system, a healthy hive, a defective wing. Judgements of natural
goodness are ordinary evaluative statements about whether a living thing is going as
its kind needs to go. Foot's thesis is that moral judgements about human beings are the
same sort of judgement. Injustice is a defect in a human being in the way that blight is
a defect in a plant — not because we dislike it, but because of what it is to be the
kind of creature we are.

The standing objection is older than both. G. E. Moore's open question argument, from
*Principia Ethica* (1903), runs: take any natural property you like — pleasure, survival,
the satisfaction of needs — and it always remains a live question whether a thing with
that property is good. If *good* just meant that property, the question would be closed,
as "is this bachelor unmarried?" is closed. It never is.

## What it suggests

If naturalism is right, moral disagreement is a disagreement about facts, and you can
be simply mistaken about ethics the way you can be mistaken about nutrition. That is a
large practical difference. It licenses moral expertise, makes moral education a matter
of getting people to see accurately rather than to feel correctly, and lets you say that
a whole culture — yours included — has been wrong for centuries. Slavery was not wrong
when we came to disapprove of it; it was wrong, and we found out.

It also promises to close the gap between the moral world and the world described by
the sciences without deflating either. Nicholas Sturgeon's "Moral Explanations" (1985)
pressed this point: we routinely explain events by citing moral facts — the regime fell
*because it was unjust*, the experiment was stopped *because it was cruel* — and such
explanations look no worse than the everyday causal explanations we accept elsewhere.

The strongest objection is not Moore's dictionary puzzle but the challenge Gilbert
Harman posed in *The Nature of Morality* (1977). Observing a scientist, you must posit
the particle to explain what she sees. Observing children setting a cat on fire, you
need only posit your own upbringing to explain what you say. The moral fact does no work
the psychological fact was not already doing. If that is right, moral properties are
idle, and idle posits are usually not real ones.

And there is the older worry that naturalism proves too little. Suppose Foot is right
that injustice is a defect in a human being. Why should a particular human being care
about being a good specimen of anything? A person can quite coherently prefer to be a
defective human and a rich one. Naturalism tells you what is wrong with him. It has more
trouble telling him.

## The problem

Nadia chairs a national committee revising the standards for residential care of
severely disabled adults. The old standards were written in 1974 around a single idea:
safety. Nothing in them is cruel. Residents are fed, clean, warm and alive, and the
inspection regime measures exactly those things.

Her researcher has come back with a report she cannot unread. In four homes, residents
have not been outdoors in over a year. Nobody has been harmed. Nobody has complained;
several of the residents cannot. Staff are kind, the buildings are compliant, and the
families are grateful. On every metric the 1974 standards contain, these homes are
excellent.

Nadia wants to write into the new code that a life without weather, without choice about
the day, without anyone who is glad to see you, is a bad life for a human being — and
that this is true whether or not the person can report it, whether or not the families
mind, whether or not any harm can be shown.

The legal advisor tells her she cannot. Harm is a fact and can be inspected. "A bad life
for a human being" is a philosophical opinion, and the sector will litigate it into the
ground. If she wants it enforceable, she must reduce it to measurable outcomes — and
Nadia knows, watching the footage of a man sitting by a window that does not open, that
the reduction will lose the thing she saw.

## The questions

**1. The welfare code.** Write an animal welfare code from what a healthy animal of the
species needs, or from what owners in the region have always accepted?

- From the species. There are facts about what a creature needs, and they do not vote.
- From measurable suffering and thriving; "flourishing" adds nothing to the data.
- From local practice. "What the animal needs" is always somebody's reading of the animal.
- Either way it is a policy, not a discovery. There are no facts about goodness to report.

- Yes, exactly. Cruelty damages what a human being is for.
- Yes, but the fact is welfare. The suffering is world enough.
- No. Lead has effects; cruelty has effects plus our condemnation, and only the effects
  are out there.
- No — the fact is too thin. Something beyond nature must make cruelty forbidden.

## Reading your answer

**Goodness Is a Fact About Kinds.** You take moral judgement to be a species of the
judgement we already make when we call a hive healthy or a wing defective. This gives you
real moral facts, real error, and real progress, with no extra furniture in the universe.
The pressure point is that human beings are the one kind whose form of life is partly
chosen, and Moore's question — is a well-functioning human necessarily a good one? — has
never quite closed.

**What Can Be Counted Is What Is Real.** You keep the naturalist's discipline and spend
it on welfare. Arguments become tractable and evidence decides them. What you cannot
reach is the wrong that leaves no trace: the undetected betrayal, the promise to the
dead, the man by the sealed window whose file records no harm.

**There Is No View From Nowhere.** You notice that every claim about what humans need is
made by someone standing somewhere. That is true and worth holding onto. It costs you the
sentence "this practice is wrong, not merely foreign" — and you should know in advance
which case will make you want that sentence back.

**The World Contains No Oughts.** You grant the naturalist's world and deny that morality
is in it. Harman's challenge is on your side and has never been cleanly answered. What you
owe is an explanation of moral argument itself: people spend enormous effort on
disagreements you say have nothing at stake, and that effort is a fact your theory has to
account for.

**Nature Is Not Enough.** You hold that description never yields prohibition, which is the
oldest and cleanest objection in this area. The price is that your grounding cannot be
exhibited to anyone outside it, which leaves you short of arguments exactly where you need
them most.

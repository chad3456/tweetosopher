## What it means

In 1902 Lenin published *What Is To Be Done?*, arguing that a workers' movement left to
itself develops only trade-union consciousness — better wages, shorter hours — and that
revolutionary understanding has to be brought to it from outside, by an organisation of
disciplined professional revolutionaries. Under a police state, he added, this was not a
preference but a condition of surviving at all. In 1904, after the split at the party's
second congress, he defended a centralised organisational model in *One Step Forward, Two
Steps Back*.

Rosa Luxemburg replied the same year, in an essay published in *Iskra* and *Neue Zeit* and
later issued in English under the title *Leninism or Marxism?* Her objection was not that
Lenin's committee would be incompetent. It was that the relationship he proposed between the
organisation and the class was the wrong way round. A working class becomes capable of ruling
by acting, arguing and making its own mistakes; the errors of a real movement, she wrote, are
more fruitful than the infallibility of the best central committee. Lenin's ultra-centralism
she described as the spirit of a night watchman rather than of a fighting movement — an
apparatus concerned above all with controlling its own members.

The paradox is in the structure, not in anyone's motives. A vanguard exists to act on behalf
of a class that is not yet in a position to act for itself. But the more effectively it acts
on their behalf, the less occasion they have to develop the capacity it was formed to
supply — and the more reason the vanguard has to conclude that they still are not ready.
There is no natural point at which it hands anything back.

Trotsky, in *Our Political Tasks* (1904) and then opposing Lenin, named the mechanism with
more precision than anyone: the party organisation substitutes itself for the party, the
central committee for the organisation, and in the end a single dictator for the central
committee. He would join the Bolsheviks three years later, which is part of what makes the
passage famous.

Luxemburg returned to it in 1918, in prison, in the manuscript published after her murder as
*The Russian Revolution*. That text is routinely misread by both sides. She defended the
Bolshevik seizure of power and attacked the social democrats who said they should have
waited. She also warned that suppressing elections, the press and open dispute would leave
not the dictatorship of a class but of a handful of politicians, and that freedom which
excludes those who think differently is not freedom.

## What it suggests

The general form of the problem is not confined to Marxism. Any body that claims to act for
people who are not in the room faces it: a party, a union, a professional association, an
NGO speaking for a community it fundraises about, a technocratic agency, a religious order, a
founder-run company insisting that its users do not know what they want. In each case the
claim to represent generates the authority, and the authority erodes the conditions under
which representation could be checked.

Robert Michels made the strongest generalisation in *Political Parties* (1911). Studying the
most internally democratic organisations available to him — German social democracy and the
trade unions — he concluded that organisation itself produces oligarchy: whoever holds the
posts accumulates expertise, continuity and control of information, and the membership
defers to them for good reasons before it defers for bad ones. If Michels is right, the
vanguard problem is not a Leninist error but the standing condition of collective action.

**The objections run in both directions, and both are serious.**

Against the vanguard: the historical record after 1917 is the argument, and it does not need
elaborating. What needs stating is that the prediction was made in advance, by participants,
on structural grounds — Luxemburg in 1904, Trotsky in 1904, Bakunin against Marx as early as
the 1870s, warning that a workers' state would be run by ex-workers looking down on the
masses from the height of the state.

Against Luxemburg: spontaneity has a record too, and it is not good. Movements that refuse
structure are not thereby leaderless. Jo Freeman's "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" (1972),
written from inside the women's liberation movement, argued that an informal elite forms
regardless, selected by friendship and free time, and that it cannot be removed because it
is not admitted to exist. On that reading the choice is never between having a vanguard and
not having one, only between an accountable one and a deniable one.

And against the tidy verdict: whether Bolshevik degeneration followed from the organisational
form or from civil war, blockade and the physical destruction of the industrial working class
is a live historical dispute — Isaac Deutscher's account puts substantial weight on the
emergency, and the question has not been settled by anyone's satisfaction with the answer.

## The problem

Farida chairs a coalition of sixty tenants' groups that has just lost a rent regulation it
spent two years on.

It lost for a reason nobody disputes. In April a compromise was available that would have
covered most of the affected buildings. The coalition argued about it until August, when the
window shut. Three groups had a veto by convention and used it; a fourth changed its mind
twice.

The proposal now before the coalition creates a strategy committee of seven with authority to
settle negotiating positions between conferences. Two of the people who would sit on it are
the best organisers Farida has ever worked with, and the proposal was written by the youngest
of them, who has read none of this history and reinvented the argument for it unaided.

Farida has three things in her head at once.

The first is that the coalition already has a leadership: her, the two organisers, and the
person who runs the mailing list, and none of them can be voted out of anything because none
of them formally holds a position.

The second is a woman from the Northfield estate who said at the last conference that she had
not understood a single agenda item and had stopped coming, and who was not being rude.

The third is that the committee will be good. It will win things. And there will come a
meeting, probably in eighteen months, where it explains why the conference cannot be told
something yet, and the reason it gives will be true.

## The questions

**1. The proposal.** A binding strategy committee, after a defeat caused by three months of
unresolved argument.

- Adopt it. A movement that cannot decide is available to be beaten.
- Reject it. A committee that binds the membership ends by replacing it.
- It only formalises the eight people who already decide and cannot be voted out.
- Any organisation of this size produces a ruling group; the question is visibility.

**2. The precedent.** A party of professional revolutionaries took power, and within five
years a dozen men decided everything.

- The outcome was in the design. Substitution runs one way.
- Circumstances did it: civil war, blockade, the destruction of the class it rested on.
- It was warned about at the time, from inside, and the mechanism was named.
- It was predicted a generation earlier, and not by a Marxist.

**3. The good committee.** It wins two campaigns, then asks to suspend the conference during
a dispute because the employer reads the minutes.

- Refuse. This is the step, and it always comes with a real operational reason.
- Agree with a fixed return date. Secrecy in a dispute is not rule by committee.
- Agree only if the membership decides it, not the committee that benefits.
- The winning record is the danger. Competence is how a leadership stops asking.

**4. The ballot.** The membership votes, informed and by a large majority, to make the
committee's authority permanent.

- Then it is legitimate. Overriding them to protect them is the thing you feared.
- A majority cannot vote away the next majority's ability to change its mind.
- Watch the committee members. Whether being obeyed still discomforts them matters more.
- Leave and organise elsewhere.

## Reading your answer

**What Actually Wins.** You judge form by what it can do under pressure, and you notice how
many movements were destroyed while still arguing. That is the strongest part of Lenin's case
and it stands free of the rest of him. The exposure is that the argument has no stopping
rule — each further concentration is justified by the reasoning that justified the last, and
the emergency renews.

**The Thing You Cannot Delegate.** You treat the capacity to elect, dispute and reverse as
not for trade, even against effectiveness. Luxemburg is with you, and she argued it while
defending the revolution rather than from the sidelines. The cost is real: you will
sometimes be beaten by an organisation that made the other choice.

**Who Authorised It.** You ask who agreed, on what terms, and whether they can withdraw,
which converts a century-old quarrel into answerable questions about mandate and recall. Its
limit is the ballot in question four: consent given once can be used to close the way back.

**What Leading Does to Leaders.** You watch what a few years of being right does to people —
the point where competence turns into entitlement. Michels found exactly that in the most
democratic organisations he could find. It is not a safeguard on its own; character does not
reliably outlast incentives.

**It Happens Anyway.** You think oligarchy forms regardless, so the choice is between a
visible one and a deniable one. Michels and Freeman make that hard to dismiss, and it stops
you believing flat structures are innocent. It shades into fatalism: if every arrangement
produces a ruling group, you have no reason to resist this one — which is the argument that
ruling group will make.

**The People It Is For.** You measure the structure by what it does to the woman from
Northfield: participant or audience. That is the difference the theoretical argument usually
loses. Its weakness is that tending the membership can quietly replace winning the thing the
membership joined for.

**Leave and Build Elsewhere.** You would rather go than be a component of something you
expect to fail. A serious position, and right more than once. It is also how a movement sheds
exactly the people who saw the problem coming.

## What it means

In 1951, in *The Origins of Totalitarianism*, Hannah Arendt looked at what had
happened to the millions of people whom the collapse of Europe's empires and the rise
of the nation-state had left without a country: the stateless, the denationalised, the
refugees whom no government would claim. What she noticed was not that their rights
were violated. It was stranger than that. They had rights, on paper, of the most
exalted kind — the rights of man, declared to belong to every human being simply as a
human being — and those rights turned out to be worth nothing precisely when a person
had nothing else.

Her explanation was that the declarations had the logic backwards. The rights of man
were supposed to belong to people in their bare humanity, independent of any
government. In practice they were guaranteed by governments, to their own members,
and a person expelled from every political community discovered that "human being" was
not a status anybody was obliged to honour. Worse, Arendt argued, such a person did not
merely lose particular protections. They lost the *place* from which a claim can be
made at all — a community in which their words count as an argument and their actions
count as an act. She called what they had lost "the right to have rights": not any
specific entitlement, but the standing to be someone who can hold entitlements, which
turns out to be prior to all of them.

The phrase is doing precise work. It is not a demand for more rights. It is the claim
that there is a condition underneath the list, and that the condition is membership in
some organised human community. Being human, Arendt wrote bleakly, was in the end no
protection: the world found nothing sacred in the abstract nakedness of being nothing
but human.

## What it suggests

If Arendt is right, then human-rights language contains a load-bearing lie, and
noticing it changes what you do. The urgent question stops being *what does every
person deserve* and becomes *who is answerable*. A charter without a court, a
declaration without a claimant, a right with no institution on the other side of it —
these are not partial victories on the way to the real thing. On this reading they are
the thing that lets everyone feel the problem has been addressed.

Taken seriously, it argues for spending scarce political energy on standing rather than
on statements: registration, legal personality, the right to appeal, the right to work,
the right to be counted in a census. Seyla Benhabib, developing Arendt in *The Rights of
Others* (2004), pushes toward rights of membership and residence as the practical form
the insight has to take. Giorgio Agamben pushes the other way, treating the stateless
person as revealing what sovereignty always was — that the state's basic power is the
power to decide who counts as merely alive rather than politically alive.

The strongest objection is that the argument proves too much and concedes too much. If
rights are real only where a polity honours them, then the person with no polity has no
rights to appeal to — which is precisely the conclusion every government that has ever
turned a boat around was hoping to reach. Defenders of natural rights reply that Arendt
has confused *having* a right with *enjoying* one: a right that is being violated is
still a right, and it is the violation that the language is for. There is also a
historical objection: since 1951 an international human-rights regime has grown teeth
of a sort — treaty bodies, regional courts, refugee conventions — and stateless people
have occasionally won in them. Not often. But the claim that the abstractly human can
never be enforced is now an empirical claim, and the record is mixed rather than empty.

## The problem

Mirela was born in a town that was in one country when she was born and in another by
the time she was six. Neither country's registry has her. Her mother's papers burned;
her father is not on any document that survives. She is thirty-one and has lived in the
same city for nineteen years.

You are the duty officer on a municipal review panel, and Mirela's file is the third
one this morning. She has been detained for four months pending removal. The removal
cannot happen: the receiving state has replied, twice, that she is not theirs. Nothing
in the file suggests she has done anything wrong. Nothing in the file suggests she can
be let go, either — released, she has no permit to work, no address that a landlord
will accept, and no entitlement to the clinic on the corner.

The lawyer sitting opposite you says the detention is unlawful. The lawyer sitting
beside you says the detention is the only thing currently keeping Mirela housed and
fed. Both of them are right, and both of them know it. The form in front of you has two
boxes.

What Mirela lacks is not any particular right. She lacks the thing that would make it
someone's job to answer her.

## The questions

**1. The undocumented man.** Eleven years in the country, no state will claim him, no
offence but his presence. What is he owed?

- Everything a citizen is owed; rights attach to persons, not to paperwork.
- Protection from harm, yes; membership, no. Those are different things.
- Whatever regularising him costs — eleven unlivable years is a harm the state inflicted.
- The standing to make a claim at all. The rest follows from that.

**2. The unenforceable charter.** Dignity, speech and security guaranteed; no court, no
police, no budget. Sign?

- Sign. Declared rights become claimable rights; the words come first.
- Refuse. A right nobody can be made to honour teaches people the language is theatre.
- Sign, but fund one enforceable thing instead. Institutions, not adjectives.
- Sign, because the people it names have nothing else, and a breakable promise beats silence.

**3. The town of nine thousand.** Four hundred stateless people, tight housing, a lawful
right to refuse.

- Take them and enrol them — schools, local votes, the right to stand for office.
- Take them as guests. Shelter is unconditional; a share in the town's future is granted.
- Take the number the town can absorb, and say plainly that the limit is real.
- Refuse and lobby for a national scheme. States made this; nine thousand people cannot fix it.

**4. The review panel.** Lawful indefinite detention, or release into a limbo with no
permit and no address.

- Release her. Indefinite detention of the innocent is not fixable by procedure.
- Release her only with support attached; freedom into destitution is another cruelty.
- Keep her detained. A panel member who decides on conscience has stopped being a panel.
- Release her and resign publicly, so the decision indicts the scheme.

## Reading your answer

**Rights Before Papers.** You hold that standing comes with being human, and that a
state issuing rights like licences has misunderstood what it is doing. Arendt agreed
with you about the principle — and spent a chapter showing that the principle fed
nobody. Your open question is who, exactly, is on the hook when no polity claims a
person.

**The Body That Guarantees.** You think a right is real only where something can be made
to honour it, so you ask who is answerable before you ask what is deserved. This is the
hard-headed Arendt, and it explains statelessness better than the alternatives. It also
makes membership prior to protection, which is the arrangement that produced the
stateless — so you owe an account of what stops the guarantor from narrowing the list.

**Capacity, Not Declaration.** You judge by whether anyone's life is different
afterwards, which keeps you honest about limits that idealists talk past. The price is
that capacity ends up setting the moral boundary, and that argument has been available
to every government that would rather not.

**The Person in Front of You.** You attend to the particular woman and what would help
her by Thursday. You catch what the legal argument misses. What you do not yet have is
a rule that scales to the four hundred who are not in the room.

**What the Office Makes of You.** You watch what the machinery does to the person inside
it, and treat public refusal as part of the job. The objection is that your seat is
filled the next morning by someone with fewer scruples.

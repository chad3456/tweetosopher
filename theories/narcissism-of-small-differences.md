## What it means

The phrase appears in *Civilization and Its Discontents* (1930), in the middle of Freud's
argument about aggression, and it takes up about a page. He is asking why civilisation
demands so much of people and why the demand is so unstable, and his answer is that human
beings are constitutionally aggressive — that the instruction to love one's neighbour is
issued against a standing inclination to exploit and humiliate him.

Civilisation therefore has to do something with the aggression it forbids. One of the
arrangements it hits on, Freud says, is convenient and comparatively harmless: **it is
always possible to bind a considerable number of people together in love, provided there
are others left over to receive the aggression.** And the others most readily available are
not distant enemies. They are the community next door — the one that speaks almost the same
language, worships in almost the same way, and can be told apart only by markers an outsider
cannot see at all.

He gives the examples that were to hand: Spaniards and Portuguese, North and South Germans,
the English and the Scots. He calls the phenomenon **the narcissism of minor differences**,
a phrase he had used at least twice before — in *The Taboo of Virginity* (1918) and *Group
Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego* (1921) — and whose underlying observation he credits
to the anthropologist Ernest Crawley, who had written about the taboos separating even
closely related peoples.

Two things are worth registering about the passage. The first is that "narcissism" is doing
real work: what is being defended is not a doctrine but a self-image, and the neighbour is
intolerable because he is a slightly wrong version of you. The second is Freud's own word
*harmless*, which he attaches to the arrangement because it discharges aggression at a low
cost. He then discusses antisemitism among Christian peoples in the same breath. He was
writing in Vienna in 1930, and he did not live long enough in that city to see how the
sentence read afterwards.

## What it suggests

**Proximity is a risk factor.** If the mechanism is real, the dangerous relationship is not
with the stranger but with the near-relation — the splinter group, the rival denomination,
the neighbouring charity, the faction that agrees with you about ninety per cent of
everything. Anton Blok's comparative survey (1998) makes the empirical case: serious violence
clusters among adjacent and similar communities, and boundaries need the most maintenance
where the underlying difference is thinnest. Pierre Bourdieu found the same shape in
*Distinction* (1979): the sharpest symbolic struggles occur between neighbouring positions in
social space.

**Solidarity may be structurally parasitic on an outsider.** This is the uncomfortable
version. If your group is at its warmest when a particular name is mentioned, the warmth and
the hostility may be one thing rather than two, and you cannot straightforwardly keep the
first while retiring the second.

**And here are the objections, which are strong.**

*The differences are not small.* "Small" is a measurement taken from outside, by someone who
does not have to live on either side. Clifford Geertz's account of primordial attachments
makes the point without any partisanship: ties of language, blood and custom are experienced
from within as coercive and ineffable, not as preferences of adjustable size. The term has a
history of being applied downwards — to sects, to colonised peoples, to any conflict the
speaker has the luxury of finding baffling — and used that way it converts a grievance into a
character flaw. The reply available to Freud is narrow but real: the claim was never that the
difference does not matter to the participants, only that the intensity of the mattering is
evidence about the group and not about the neighbour.

*The mechanism is wrong even if the pattern is right.* Freud's explanation depends on a
drive theory — aggression as a quantity requiring discharge — that almost nobody now accepts.
Erich Fromm's *The Anatomy of Human Destructiveness* (1973) rejects the innate death drive
and argues that destructiveness is a character formation produced by particular social
conditions, which makes it changeable rather than a constant to be drained somewhere.

*And it may be a psychological name for a material fight.* Charles Tilly's *Durable
Inequality* (1998) argues that categorical boundaries persist because they organise access to
resources. On that reading two organisations with a doctrinal split and one contract between
them do not need any account of narcissism at all.

## The problem

Ruth has run the Eastgate Trust for fourteen years. It provides respite sitting for people
caring for a relative at home. A mile away, Kirkhill Care does the same thing, for the same
families, from a building Ruth has never entered.

The two organisations differ on one question. Eastgate uses trained volunteers, on the view
that the arrangement is sustainable and that neighbours should do this for each other.
Kirkhill uses paid staff, on the view that unpaid sitting is unreliable, unaccountable, and
quietly extracted from women. Both positions have serious people behind them. Both have
evidence. Nobody outside the sector can hold the distinction in their head for more than a
minute.

They have not shared a platform in eleven years. Between them they run two finance systems,
two sets of insurance, two waiting lists — and a family that phones the wrong one is told,
politely, to phone the other.

Now a foundation has offered three years of unrestricted funding on condition that they
merge. Ruth has done the arithmetic and it would clear both waiting lists inside a year.

What has stopped her sleeping is something else. In Tuesday's staff meeting somebody made
the usual joke about Kirkhill, and the room lit up — people volunteering for shifts,
covering for each other, laughing. Ruth has spent fourteen years building that, and she
watched it arrive, in about four seconds, from a joke about a charity a mile away.

## The questions

**1. The merger.** Three years of funding, conditional on merging.

- Merge. The disagreement is real and it is not worth what it has cost the families.
- Refuse. That difference is the method, and it is called small by people who have never
  done the work.
- Merge the back office; keep both practices running, documented rather than resolved.
- The offer is the problem: a funder settling a professional disagreement with money.

**2. The meeting.** The room is never warmer than after a joke about the other charity.

- That is the point, and it is a reason to distrust the enmity rather than enjoy it.
- Cohesion from rivalry is ordinary and useful; organisations without it go limp.
- The cohesion is real and so is the bill, paid by families nobody names in that room.
- Calling it psychology flatters everyone; both are bidding for one contract.

**3. The word.** Calling a difference small is done by people who do not live on either side
of it.

- She is right; the term converts a grievance into a personality defect.
- That mistakes the claim — it is about what the mattering is doing, not whether it matters.
- The pattern is real and the drive theory explaining it is not.
- It describes something documented: violence clusters among neighbours, not strangers.

**4. The new board.** A proposal to exclude the other founder over something she wrote nine
years ago.

- Support it. Treating a practice disagreement as a personality clash is how bad practice
  survives.
- Oppose it. The merged body has found its stranger within four minutes.
- Neither side here includes anyone who uses the service. Ask them, and be bound.
- Purging someone over a nine-year-old position tells everyone else what awaits them.

## Reading your answer

**What It Costs Outside the Room.** You ask what the division produces for people who are
not in it, and you will spend a loyalty to shorten a waiting list. That is Freud with the
most practical purchase. The same reasoning will eventually trade away a distinction that
was carrying real information, and you will find out only when the service is worse.

**The Difference Is Real.** You refuse to have a considered position reclassified as a
symptom, and that move is available against any conviction including the one dissolving
yours. From the inside it is indistinguishable from the thing Freud described, and everyone
in an eleven-year feud believes their difference is the substantive one.

**What the Feud Makes of You.** You watch what the rivalry does to the people in it — the
warmth that arrives with the joke, the speed of the first exclusion. That is the register
Freud was writing in. It does not settle whether the position being purged was wrong.

**Ask the People It Is For.** You want the decision put to the people on the receiving end,
which replaces a contest of self-descriptions with an answerable question. Those people
depend on both organisations and know it, which makes their answer harder to trust than the
procedure assumes.

**Whose Difference Is Called Small.** You are alert to who gets to call a grievance trivial,
and the word is almost always applied downwards. Applied generally, the corrective protects
every faction from every diagnosis, and nothing can then be said about a feud from outside
one.

**Nobody Is Standing Outside.** You distrust the vantage point the term requires and suspect
everyone of describing the conflict in terms that suit their position in it. Applied to
yourself, it leaves you no ground for saying the eleven years were a mistake — which you
plainly think they were.

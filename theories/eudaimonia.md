## What it means

*Eudaimonia* is the word Aristotle uses for what everyone is ultimately after, and
"happiness" is a poor translation of it. Happiness in English names a feeling. Eudaimonia
names a way a life is going — closer to "flourishing", or to what we mean when we say
somebody is doing well, meaning not that they are cheerful today but that their life is
in good shape. It is assessed over a whole life, not a moment; Aristotle quotes the
proverb that one swallow does not make a summer.

His argument for what it consists in is the function argument, in Book I of the
*Nicomachean Ethics*. A good flautist is one who flutes well; a good eye is one that
sees well; anything with a characteristic activity is good when it performs that
activity well. Human beings, he claims, have a characteristic activity too: not growth,
which plants share, nor perception, which animals share, but rational activity. So the
human good is the activity of the soul in accordance with virtue — reason exercised
excellently, over a complete life.

Three features of the account do most of the work. First, it is *activity*, not a state
of mind: you cannot flourish while asleep, and Aristotle explicitly denies that a person
who has virtue but never exercises it is living well. Second, it needs *external goods*.
This is where he parts company with the Stoics, and it is the least comfortable part of
his view: he says that flourishing can be damaged by poverty, illness, ugliness, low
birth, bad children, and the death of friends. Virtue is necessary and not sufficient.
Priam, who lost everything at the end of a long and admirable life, is not called happy.
Third, in Book X he raises contemplation above the practical virtues as the highest
activity, which sits oddly with the rest of the book and has divided commentators ever
since.

## What it suggests

If eudaimonia is the right target, then the standard modern question — are you happy? —
is the wrong one, or at least a badly aimed one. The right question is whether you are
doing anything, whether the capacities you have are being used, and whether the shape of
your life over decades is one you could recognise as going well. Satisfaction surveys
would not settle it. A person can report high satisfaction while doing nothing at all,
and Aristotle's whole point is that this is a coherent thing to be and not a good one.

This has a political edge that Martha Nussbaum has made central. If flourishing requires
external goods — nutrition, bodily security, education, the ability to form attachments,
the capacity to participate in political life — then a society's job is to secure the
conditions under which people can exercise their capabilities, and the measure of a
country is not its aggregate wealth or its reported happiness but what its people are
actually able to do and to be.

It also supplies the best-known argument against pure hedonism, which Robert Nozick made
vivid. If all that mattered were experience, you should plug into a machine that supplies
any experience you like, forever, with no memory of having chosen. Most people refuse,
and the interesting question is what the refusal reveals: apparently we want to *do*
things and *be* certain ways, not merely to have the corresponding experiences.

The objections are serious. The function argument is the weakest link: the inference from
"humans are the animals that reason" to "the human good is reasoning well" needs the
premise that having a distinctive capacity makes exercising it your good, and that
premise is doing a lot of unexamined work. Knives have functions because someone made
them for something; it is not obvious that species do. And the resulting picture of the
flourishing life — leisured, contemplative, self-sufficient, magnanimous, uninterested in
manual work, silent about the labour that made all this possible — looks a great deal
like a portrait of the class Aristotle belonged to. Julia Annas has argued that the
ancient eudaimonist framework survives these charges better than it looks; but anyone
using it has to be able to say why their account of human flourishing is not simply a
description of the life they already wanted.

## The problem

Yusuf is sixty-one and has spent eleven years caring for his mother.

Before that he was a structural engineer, competent rather than distinguished, and he
had been putting money aside for a workshop — he restores clocks, badly at first and now
very well, and there is a specific eighteenth-century longcase movement he has been
promising himself since 2009.

His mother has advanced dementia. She has not known who he is since 2019. The care is
mostly physical now: lifting, feeding, the four-hourly turn, a night that gets broken
twice. He has a sister in Toronto who sends money and calls on Sundays and would not
pretend to be doing more.

A social worker has told him, kindly and more than once, that there is funding for a
residential place, that it is a good home, that he has done more than anyone could ask,
and that at sixty-one his own shoulder is not going to hold much longer. She used the
phrase "your own life", and he has been turning it over for a week, because he cannot
work out whether it refers to something real.

By Aristotle's account, Yusuf is not flourishing. He is not exercising his capacities.
He has no leisure, no politics, no friendships that survived, and the activity that
fills his days is the same three tasks repeated. By his own account, when he is honest,
he is doing the only thing available that he would still respect himself for having
done — and he does not think a man who put his mother in a home to restore clocks would
be flourishing either, whatever the philosophy said.

He would like to know whether the last eleven years were a life or an interruption to
one, and he is aware that the answer determines whether he makes the call.

## Reading your answer

**A Life Is Something You Do.** You hold that living well is activity rather than
feeling, assessed over decades, and that a contented person can be living badly. This is
why the machine repels you. Its cost is a standard you cannot fully justify to the
person it convicts: you are telling someone their life is worse than they take it to be,
on the strength of a picture of human function they may not share.

**Nobody Else Grades It.** You distrust any account of wellbeing that lets a philosopher
overrule the person living the life, and you have noticed how neatly Aristotle's
flourishing resembles Aristotle's own preferences. This keeps you honest about
paternalism. Your exposure is the machine: if only the inside counts, it is a good deal,
and almost nobody takes it — which suggests people want something your theory says they
cannot want.

**The Conditions Are the Question.** You seize on the dependence of flourishing on
circumstance and conclude that securing the conditions is the main event, not charity.
Nussbaum built a development economics out of this move. Where it strains is the single
case: you can say a great deal about what a society owes a person and rather less about
what Yusuf should do on Monday.

**The Life Has Other People In It.** You keep returning to whoever else is inside the
life being assessed — the children, the mother, the people outside the tank. This is a
real correction, since Aristotle's flourishing is strikingly singular and his good man
is nobody's dependant. The cost is that it can swallow a person whole: every obligation
you name is real, and a life composed only of them is not one Aristotle would have
called good either.

**Whose Idea of a Good Life.** You read the *Ethics* as a portrait rather than a
discovery, which is fair and standard. What it leaves you with is thin. You can say an
account is parochial, but you still have to live, and a settled refusal to rank lives
makes it hard to explain why anyone should be lifted out of a bad one.

**There Is No Function.** You deny that people have a characteristic activity the way a
knife has a use, so the forty-eight-year-old is not discovering what his life is for but
making it. This is the cleanest available break with Aristotle. It also removes any
ground for telling someone they wasted theirs — and it is worth checking whether you
have ever wanted to say that.

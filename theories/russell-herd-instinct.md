## What it means

Bertrand Russell gave the first BBC Reith Lectures in 1948, published the following year
as *Authority and the Individual*. The lectures ask a question he had been circling since
*Principles of Social Reconstruction* (1916): human beings evolved in small bands where
loyalty inward and suspicion outward were survival traits, and they now live in states of
tens of millions where those same dispositions are catastrophic. The impulses did not
update. They were merely handed larger instruments.

Russell's specific claim — the one that makes this more than a truism about tribalism — is
about **pleasure**. He noticed that people in a crowd acting together are not grimly doing
their duty. They are having the best time they have had in years. Ordinary modern life,
he argued, is safe, repetitive and short on drama, and collective excitement supplies
exactly what it lacks: intensity, the sense of mattering, the dissolution of the private
self into something larger. This is why the outbreak of a war is so often greeted with
celebration in the streets, and why the celebration is not hypocrisy. The people cheering
mean it.

Two consequences follow, and Russell pressed both. The first is that collective
excitement **suspends individual judgement**. Not corrupts — suspends. In the crowd the
question of whether the thing being demanded is true or fair does not so much get answered
wrongly as fail to come up, because the feeling of certainty is already fully supplied by
everyone else's presence. The second, developed further in the essays collected as
*Unpopular Essays* (1950), is that a great deal of cruelty is committed by people who are
enjoying themselves — not sadists, not fanatics, but ordinary people at a pleasant
occasion whose ordinariness is the whole problem.

Russell did not conclude that assembly should be discouraged, and it would have been
strange if he had: he spent his eighties organising mass civil disobedience against
nuclear weapons and went to prison for it at eighty-nine. His prescription in the Reith
lectures is that a society must provide outlets for these impulses which are not
destructive, because the impulses are not going to be reasoned away.

## What it suggests

If Russell is right, the test of a political feeling is not its content but its texture.
The relevant question about a conviction is not only *is this true* but *did the certainty
arrive before the argument did*. That is an unwelcome question, because it is answerable,
and because most people can remember the moment.

It also relocates the study of atrocity. If cruelty at scale mostly requires enthusiastic
ordinary participants rather than monsters, then the protective factor is not moral
instruction but something closer to a habit of remaining slightly outside the mood of the
room. Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992) found something close to this in a
reserve police battalion: what moved the men was not ideology but the situation and the
wish not to let down the others.

**The strongest objection is that this is a temperament dressed as an analysis.** Every
abolitionist meeting, every general strike, every march that ever won anything ran on
precisely the collective excitement Russell is treating as a warning light. Émile
Durkheim's *Elementary Forms of the Religious Life* (1912) argued that assembled
excitement is the origin of moral obligation itself rather than a solvent of it, and
Barbara Ehrenreich's *Dancing in the Streets* (2006) argues that the long suspicion of
collective joy has served whoever preferred people at home. On that reading, the detached
observer who never quite joins in is not being careful. He is being comfortable, and
calling it a virtue.

Russell's honest position leaves both standing: the mechanism that produced the Aldermaston
marches and the mechanism that produced the pogrom are the same mechanism. Which means it
cannot be used as a guide, in either direction.

## The problem

Marta is a nurse in a river town of forty thousand. Nine months ago a storage tank at the
chemical works leaked into the water and children got sick; the company paid a fine
smaller than its legal fees and the plant manager, Halvard Ek, still drives to work past
the school.

For three weeks now people have gathered outside the company office at seven in the
evening. Marta went on the third night, expecting to feel worthy and slightly cold. What
she felt instead was awake. She has not felt this awake since her twenties. By the second
week she was thinking about seven o'clock from four o'clock, and she has noticed that the
part of the evening she likes best is the chanting, which is also the part where she could
not say what is being chanted without stopping to listen.

Her colleague Piotr thinks the analysis is beside the point. Four years of letters got
nothing; three weeks of noise got a lawyer to the door. On the seventh evening of the
third week somebody put a paving stone through a window on Halvard Ek's street — the wrong
house, as it turned out, his mother's. Marta was on shift that night. She had, two weeks
earlier, helped print the flyer with the street name on it.

She has to decide whether she is going tonight.

**1. The thrill.** You notice you are enjoying the crowd, and the excitement arrived

- Treat the pleasure as a warning about the conviction attached to it.

- The pleasure is the mechanism. Nothing else has ever built a movement.
- The feeling is neutral; ask what this crowd will do on Thursday.

**2. The organiser.** Four years of letters got nothing; three weeks of noise got an
answer.

- He is right about the leverage, and that settles it.
- Keep turning up, and keep saying the doubts aloud inside the crowd.
- Stop going. You are training yourself to feel certain in a room without disagreement.
- Go, because they are your neighbours and they will remember who came.

**3. The window.** A stone through the wrong house, and you printed the flyer.

- You are implicated; the number of other contributors makes it worse, not lighter.
- The thrower is responsible, and spreading the blame protects him.
- The organisers are responsible; they knew what the seventh night looks like.
- It is the price of the only pressure available to people with no lawyers.

**4. The objection.** Every movement you admire ran on this same excitement.

- The objection lands; suspicion of crowds mostly protects whoever is winning.
- Both are true — the same mechanism makes the march and the lynching.
- Good movements were good for what they demanded, not for how they felt.
- You cannot settle it from outside. You find out by being in it and staying awake.

## Reading your answer

**Awake in the Room.** You audit your own enthusiasm first, which is what Russell actually
recommends and is harder than it sounds, since the audit has to run while the pleasure is
happening. The price is a permanent half-step of distance from the people beside you.

**Ask What It Moved.** You judge a gathering by what it extracts and refuse to treat its
emotional register as morally informative. Clean — and it licenses the effective mob just
as readily, so you need a stopping rule the calculation does not supply.

**The Line Does Not Move at Night.** What may be done to a person does not change because
many people want it at once. That is stable. It also makes you frequently useless to the
people you agree with, and the demand for tidy pressure tends to appear only when the
powerless start applying it.

**These Are Your Neighbours.** Turning up is something you owe particular people, not a
position you hold; Durkheim and Ehrenreich are behind you. The obligation is local, and a
crowd's warmth toward its own has historically been hard to separate from its coldness
toward everyone else.

**Somebody Threw It.** You keep responsibility attached to an agent, which stops you
laundering your conduct through the mood of the evening. Its blind spot is Russell's
point: if the seventh night reliably produces the stone, the agent's solitary freedom is
not explaining much.
